The task

Chemists did not wake up one day with a periodic table. It took about a century of noticing patterns, arguing, being wrong and being proved right.

You are the exhibit designers. Produce a poster for a school science corridor that shows how the table was built and why it looks the way it does today. A Year 9 student should be able to read it and come away understanding how the table lets chemists predict the properties of elements.

This connects directly to what 0654 asks you to know: that the table classifies elements, that groups and periods tell you about properties, and that it can be used to predict. The history is the story that makes those ideas stick.

Poster brief

Format
A3 paper, or one digital slide at A3 proportions.
Audience
Students one year younger than you.
Must show
A timeline, one gap-and-prediction case study, the switch from mass to proton number, and a labelled piece of the modern table.
Words
Around 250 in total. Use labels, diagrams and short captions, not paragraphs.
Hand in
The poster and a source list on the back or in the notes.

Process

Follow these in order. Each step ends with something you can show your teacher.

  1. Choose your case files

    Pick one or two case files below and become the expert on them. Show your teacher which ones you have chosen.

  2. Investigate your files

    Answer every question in your chosen files in your own words. Use at least two sources, and make sure one is not Wikipedia. Keep a note of each source as you go.

  3. Explain it in two minutes

    Explain each scientist's idea to a partner or your teacher: what it got right and where it failed. Ask them to say "what problem did this solve?" until your answer is clear.

  4. Sketch, then check the science

    Draw a rough layout on paper first. Then check every claim against the case files and the checklist further down. Ask a classmate to find one thing that is unclear.

  5. Make the poster and list your sources

    Credit every image. Use the judging table to review your own work before you hand it in.

Case files

Seven turning points, in date order. Research one or two of them in depth. Your poster timeline needs to show at least three.

1829Döbereiner

Elements in threes

Johann Döbereiner noticed that some elements with similar properties came in groups of three, with the middle one sitting between the other two in mass.

  • What is a triad? Name at least two examples.
  • Why did this idea only take chemists so far?
  • Which modern group does the chlorine, bromine, iodine triad belong to?
Test it yourself. Find the relative atomic masses of chlorine and iodine, and average them. How close is that to bromine? Put your result on the poster.
Döbereiner (Wikipedia) History of the periodic table (Wikipedia)
1864Newlands

The law of octaves

John Newlands listed the elements in order of atomic mass and spotted that every eighth element seemed to repeat the properties of the first. He compared it to notes in a musical scale.

  • What was the pattern, and for roughly how many elements did it work?
  • Why was the idea laughed at when he presented it?
  • What did he do wrong that Mendeleev later did right?
Newlands (Wikipedia) RSC Periodic Table
1869Mendeleev, with Meyer working alongside

The table that left gaps

Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the known elements by atomic mass and by similar properties. His key move was to trust the pattern over the data.

  • What two rules did he use to place an element?
  • Where did he leave gaps, and why was that a bold choice?
  • Pick one gap. What did he predict? What was found, by whom, and when? Show predicted against real values in a small table on the poster.
  • Where did his table look wrong, and what did he think was the reason?
Poster requirement. The gap-and-prediction case study is a must. The hero tiles at the top of this page show two examples. Choose a different one if you can, such as eka-boron.
Mendeleev (Wikipedia) Periodic table (Britannica) RSC Periodic Table
1894Rayleigh and Ramsay

A whole new column

A tiny difference in the density of nitrogen led to the discovery of argon. Over the following years William Ramsay and colleagues found more inert gases that nobody had predicted.

  • How did chemists first realise there was something new in air?
  • Why was it hard to find a place for these gases at first?
  • How did the table cope? Call the new group Group VIII or Group 0, and explain why the number is used.
Ramsay (Wikipedia) RSC Periodic Table
1913Moseley

Mass is out, proton number is in

Henry Moseley studied the X-rays given off by different elements and found a number that increased in steps of exactly one from element to element.

  • What did he measure and how did it lead to atomic number?
  • Some pairs of elements, such as argon and potassium, were in the "wrong" order by mass. How did his work fix this?
  • Why does ordering by proton number make sense for the modern definition of an element?
Poster requirement. Include a box called "Why proton number?" that a Year 9 student could follow in under a minute.
Moseley (Wikipedia) History of the periodic table (Wikipedia)
1940sSeaborg

Why two rows hang below the table

Glenn Seaborg worked on the heavy, man-made elements and argued that they did not belong where the table then placed them.

  • What was his proposal, and what does it explain about the shape of the table?
  • How are these elements made, and why are they so unstable?
  • An element was later named in his honour while he was alive. Which one, and why is that unusual?
Seaborg (Wikipedia) RSC Periodic Table
2016IUPAC

Row seven is full

Four new elements were confirmed and named, completing the seventh period of the table.

  • Which four elements, and what are their atomic numbers?
  • Who decides when a new element is real and what it is called?
  • Sketch where an element 119 would go. What group would you expect it to be in, and why?

How your poster will be judged

Story and order Events are listed with dates. Events are in order and link to each other. Shows how each idea solved the last one's problem.
