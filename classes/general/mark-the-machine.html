It predicts words. It doesn't look things up.

A large language model writes one small piece of text at a time. At every step it scores every possible next piece, turns the scores into chances, picks one, and repeats. Nothing in that loop checks whether the result is true. Build a sentence yourself and see.

1. Text becomes tokens

The model doesn't see words. It sees tokens: whole words, parts of words or punctuation.

The first person on the Moon
unbelievable

2. Every token gets a score

Training on huge amounts of text taught it which tokens tend to follow which. A score is how well a token fits the pattern, not how true it is.

3. Temperature sets the risk

Scores become chances. Low temperature makes the top choice almost certain. High temperature flattens the chances, so unlikely words get picked more often. At 0 it always picks the top one, right or wrong.

chance ∝ escore ÷ temperature

4. Pick, add, repeat

The chosen token is added to the text and the whole loop runs again. Once a wrong word is in, the model builds on it smoothly: "Buzz" makes "Aldrin" almost certain.

Turning the temperature down doesn't make it true

Set temperature to 0 and the model always takes its top choice. That only helps if the top choice is true. Try the other prompts at 0: the Great Wall is "visible from space" and goldfish remember for "three seconds" every time, because those myths are all over the internet, so they score highest. Ask for a source and it invents one every time, because a reference-shaped answer is the likeliest text. Low temperature makes a model consistent, not correct.

It also explains why asking the same question twice can give different answers, and why an AI that is right nine times out of ten can still be confidently wrong on the tenth.

Green, amber or red?

Decide for each case, then see the reasoning. Rules differ between schools, subjects and exam boards: for coursework and anything assessed, your school's policy and your exam board's rules decide.

What Cambridge International says

Cambridge's policy on generative AI in coursework covers all Cambridge International qualifications. The short version: AI use is allowed in limited ways if you acknowledge it. Using it to create or improve your work without saying so can be treated as plagiarism.

Allowed, if clearly acknowledged

Initial research, like consulting a book or website. You must state the prompt or prompts you used, the AI tool and its version, and when you used it. Check any websites it gives you, and discuss how reliable the material is.

A brief quotation of AI-generated text in an essay, about two or three sentences (around 50 words), clearly marked as AI and then discussed critically. You only get credit for your own comments on it.

Always

  • Acknowledge any AI you used to research, plan, or create text, images, sound or video, and reference what it produced.
  • Remember that using AI earns no extra marks, and you don't have to use it at all.
  • Check its sources, because AI can invent sources that don't exist.

Not allowed

Using AI to create or improve your work without acknowledging it. Your teacher has to confirm that your coursework is your own original, unaided work. If they suspect inappropriate AI use, they should not sign it off. Cambridge can treat it as malpractice, investigate, and apply penalties.

What makes a teacher look twice

Cambridge gives teachers warning signs. None of them proves AI use, but each one can prompt questions: a sudden jump in quality between drafts, writing that doesn't sound like you, unusually perfect spelling and grammar, American spelling you don't normally use, no references from after 2021, paragraphs that start "However," or "In conclusion,", and strong evidence with weak analysis.

Check before you trust: SIFT

A fact-checking routine designed for the web by Mike Caulfield. It works just as well on a chatbot's answer, because a chatbot isn't a source: it's a summary of who knows what.

S

Stop

Notice how the answer makes you feel. Does it say exactly what you hoped? Is it very confident? Slow down before you copy it.

I

Investigate the source

Ask "where does this come from?" If the AI names a paper or book, find it yourself. If you can't, treat it as invented.

F

Find better coverage

Look for the same claim in your textbook, a specification, a reputable organisation or a peer-reviewed paper.

T

Trace claims

Follow numbers and quotes back to the original. Check units, dates and who actually said or did it.

Prompts that make you think more, not less

The best uses make AI a sparring partner. You stay the author.

Instead of "Write my essay on the causes of WW1."
Try "Here's my plan for an essay on the causes of WW1. Which counter-argument have I ignored?"

Instead of "Answer this past-paper question."
Try "Ask me five questions on the topic I'm revising, one at a time, and don't give answers until I've tried."

Instead of "Is this true?"
Try "What would I need to check to know if this is true, and where would I find it?"

What it costs that you can't see

Bias

Models learn from human text, including its stereotypes and blind spots. Ask for "a picture of a scientist" or "a typical nurse" and notice who appears. Ask whose viewpoint is missing.

Privacy

What you type may be stored, reviewed or used to improve the product, depending on the tool and its settings. Don't paste other people's personal details, private messages, or anything you'd not want read aloud.

Your own learning

Getting an answer isn't learning it. If AI does the struggle for you, the exam hall is where you'll find out. Use it to test yourself, explain, and give feedback, then do the work.

Sycophancy

Chatbots often agree with you and praise your work. Ask "what's wrong with this?" and "argue the other side" to get something useful.

Deepfakes

Realistic fake images, audio and video are easy to make. Before you share something shocking, check whether reliable news outlets are reporting it.

Energy and water

Training and running large models uses a lot of electricity and data-centre cooling. Not a reason to never use AI; a reason not to use it for things a quick search or your own head can do.
