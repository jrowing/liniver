Mechanics, materials and waves

1. Projectile motion — worked example then you try Problem set

A ball is launched horizontally from a cliff at 15 m s⁻¹ and lands 45 m from the base.

Worked example — find the height of the cliff
① Horizontal: x = u_x t → 45 = 15t → t = 3.0 s
② Vertical (u_y = 0): h = ½gt² → h = ½ × 9.81 × 9.0
③ h = 44 m

Electricity

4. Kirchhoff's laws — worked example then you try Problem set
Worked example — two-loop circuit
EMF₁ = 12 V, EMF₂ = 6 V, R₁ = 4 Ω, R₂ = 2 Ω, R₃ = 6 Ω. Find all branch currents.
① KCL at node A: I₁ = I₂ + I₃
② KVL loop 1: 12 = 4I₁ + 2I₂
③ KVL loop 2: 6 = 2I₂ − 6I₃ → solve simultaneously
Result: I₁ = 1.8 A, I₂ = 2.4 A, I₃ = −0.6 A (negative → assumed direction wrong)

Mathematical skills for A-level Physics

7. Logarithms — essential for Year 13 Problem set
Worked example — radioactive decay and ln
N = N₀ e^(−λt). Taking natural log: ln N = ln N₀ − λt
So a graph of ln N vs t is a straight line, gradient = −λ, y-intercept = ln N₀.
This is how we linearise exponential data in practicals.
Q7a
A capacitor discharges through a resistor: V = V₀ e^(−t/RC). Show how to plot a straight-line graph from this equation and state what the gradient represents.
[3]
Q7b
Solve for t: 8 = 24 e^(−t/40). Give your answer to 3 significant figures.
[2]
Q7c
Using the data below, plot ln(count rate) vs time and use the gradient to find the decay constant λ and hence the half-life of the source.
t / min: 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25
C / min⁻¹: 960, 640, 427, 285, 190, 127
[5]
Q7c answer
ln values: 6.87, 6.46, 6.06, 5.65, 5.25, 4.84
Gradient ≈ −0.0823 min⁻¹ = −λ
t½ = ln2 / λ = 0.693 / 0.0823 ≈ 8.4 min

Year 13 topic preview

10. Gravitational fields — first look Reading

In Year 13 you'll move from "g = 9.81 m s⁻²" to a full field model. Before term starts, look up gravitational field strength g and gravitational potential φ.

  • Write down (with units) what g and φ represent physically — not just the equations.
  • Explain why g is always positive but φ is always negative for a planet.
  • Use g = GM/r² to estimate g at the surface of the Moon (look up M and r).
The relationship g = −dφ/dr will be new — you don't need to use it yet, but notice that g is the negative gradient of the φ vs r graph. We'll build this up in September.

Practical skills and data analysis

13. Home experiment — timing a pendulum Investigative

You need: a piece of string (0.5–1 m), a small mass (key, nut), a ruler, and a phone timer.

  • Measure T for at least 6 different lengths. Time 10 swings each time to reduce random error.
  • Plot T² vs L. Explain why this gives a straight line, and find g from the gradient.
  • Estimate uncertainties in L and T, and hence in g. Comment on your value.
  • Identify one systematic and one random source of error in your method.
Theory reminder
T = 2π√(L/g) → T² = (4π²/g) × L
Gradient of T² vs L graph = 4π²/g

Thermal physics

16. Ideal gas law — worked example then you try Problem set
Worked example
A gas occupies 2.0 × 10⁻³ m³ at 300 K and 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa. Find the number of moles.
pV = nRT → n = pV/RT = (1.0×10⁵ × 2.0×10⁻³) / (8.31 × 300) = 0.080 mol
Q16a
A sealed container holds 0.050 mol of ideal gas at 27 °C and 1.5 × 10⁵ Pa. Find the volume of the container.
[3]
Q16b
The container is heated to 127 °C at constant volume. Calculate the new pressure, and explain in terms of molecular motion why it increases.
[4]
Q16c
Show that the root-mean-square speed of nitrogen molecules (M = 0.028 kg mol⁻¹) at 300 K is approximately 516 m s⁻¹. State one assumption of the kinetic model you used.
[4]
Hint
Use c_rms = √(3RT/M)
17. Specific heat capacity — home experiment Investigative

You need: a kettle (or saucepan), a thermometer, a known mass of water, and a timer.

  • Measure how long it takes to heat 0.5 kg of water from room temperature to 60 °C.
  • Use the kettle's rated power (printed on the base, usually 2000–3000 W) to calculate the energy supplied.
  • Calculate the specific heat capacity of water from your data. Compare with 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Your value will likely be too high. Explain why, and suggest two ways to improve the experiment.
Key equation
Q = mcΔθ and Q = Pt → c = Pt / mΔθ

Quantum physics and nuclear

19. Photoelectric effect — worked example then you try Problem set
Worked example
Light of wavelength 250 nm hits a metal with work function 3.0 eV.
Energy of photon: E = hc/λ = (6.63×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸) / (250×10⁻⁹) = 4.97 eV
Max KE: KE_max = hf − φ = 4.97 − 3.0 = 1.97 eV

[3]
Q19c

20. Energy levels and spectra — reading and reflection Reading

Look up a diagram of the hydrogen energy levels (the Bohr model). You'll find one in your textbook or at HyperPhysics.

  • Explain why emission spectra consist of discrete lines rather than a continuous spread of colours.
  • The Lyman series (transitions to n = 1) produces UV, while the Balmer series (to n = 2) produces visible light. Explain why, using the energy level diagram.
  • Calculate the wavelength of the photon emitted when an electron in hydrogen falls from n = 3 to n = 2. Use E₃ = −1.51 eV and E₂ = −3.40 eV.
  • Stretch: why does the emission spectrum of helium look different from hydrogen's, even though helium also emits visible light?
21. Radioactive decay — data analysis Past paper style

[2]
Q21b
A sample initially contains 5.0 × 10¹⁸ atoms of a radioisotope with half-life 6.0 hours. Calculate (i) the decay constant, (ii) the initial activity, (iii) the number of atoms remaining after 24 hours.
[6]
Q21c
Explain why it is not possible to say when a specific nucleus will decay, yet the decay of a large sample follows a precise exponential law.
[3]
Q21b answer
(i) λ = ln2 / t½ = 0.693 / (6 × 3600) = 3.21 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹
(ii) A₀ = λN₀ = 3.21×10⁻⁵ × 5.0×10¹⁸ = 1.61×10¹⁴ Bq
(iii) 24 hours = 4 half-lives → N = 5.0×10¹⁸ / 2⁴ = 3.1×10¹⁷
22. Wave-particle duality — investigative reading Investigative

Research the double-slit experiment with electrons (Jönsson, 1961) and with single electrons (Tonomura, 1989).

  • Describe what is observed and why it is surprising.
  • Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through 50 V. Compare this to the wavelength of visible light.
  • Explain what this tells us about the nature of electrons.
  • Reflection: in what sense can a single electron "interfere with itself"? What does this imply about the meaning of the wavefunction?
This is one of the deepest results in physics — you are not expected to resolve the philosophical puzzle, just to engage with it carefully.

23. Electric fields — worked example then you try Problem set
Worked example — field between parallel plates
Two plates separated by 4.0 mm have a p.d. of 200 V between them.
Field strength: E = V/d = 200 / 4.0×10⁻³ = 5.0×10⁴ V m⁻¹
Force on charge q = 3.2×10⁻¹⁹ C: F = qE = 1.6×10⁻¹⁴ N

Motion, momentum, and energy

26. Momentum and collisions — worked example then you try Problem set
Worked example — perfectly inelastic collision
Mass 3.0 kg at 4.0 m s⁻¹ hits stationary mass 2.0 kg. They stick together.
Conservation of momentum: 3.0 × 4.0 = 5.0 × v → v = 2.4 m s⁻¹
KE before: 24 J  ·  KE after: 14.4 J  ·  KE lost: 9.6 J → inelastic

[3]
Q26b
Show whether the collision in Q26a is elastic or inelastic.
[3]
Q26c

27. Energy conservation — investigative Investigative

Drop a ball from a measured height h and record how high h' it bounces using slow-motion video on your phone.

  • Measure h and h' for at least 5 different drop heights. Tabulate your results.

  • Where does the "lost" energy go? Describe what you observe experimentally that supports your answer.

29. Fermi estimation — order-of-magnitude thinking Investigative

For each question below, make a reasoned estimate, showing your assumptions clearly. Aim for an answer within a factor of 10 of the real value.

Q29a
How many air molecules are in this classroom right now? State any dimensions you assume.
Q29b
Estimate the power output of the Sun, using only the fact that 1 kW m⁻² of solar radiation reaches the Earth's surface and the Earth–Sun distance (~1.5 × 10¹¹ m).
Q29c

Q29d

Fermi questions appear in Paper 4 and in university physics interviews. The method — identify the key quantities, estimate each, combine — is more important than the numerical answer.
